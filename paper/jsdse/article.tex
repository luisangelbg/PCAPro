\begin{abstract}
Principal component analysis is rarely misapplied at the eigendecomposition.
It goes wrong at the decisions surrounding it: how missing values are handled,
whether variables are standardised, whether the correlation structure justifies
the analysis at all, how many components to retain, and which loadings may be
interpreted. General-purpose statistical software computes the quantities needed
to make those decisions and then stops, which is reasonable for a statistician
and is where students and non-specialist researchers go wrong. We describe
PCAPro, a platform that places the methodological argument next to the number
that justifies it. It reports eight retention criteria side by side rather than
a single answer, states which assumption is at risk and what to do about it, and
declines to present a decision as more certain than it is. PCAPro runs entirely
in a web browser with no installation, which makes it usable in shared computer
laboratories where students cannot install software, and its interface, embedded
guidance and generated report exist in full in Spanish as well as English. We
describe the pedagogical design, the implementation and its verification against
established software, and discuss the limitations of an approach that guides
without assessing.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} principal component analysis; statistics education;
multivariate statistics; educational software; data literacy; Spanish-language
resources

\section{Introduction}

Principal component analysis \citep{pearson1901, hotelling1933, jolliffe2002,
jolliffe2016, greenacre2022} is among the most widely used multivariate
techniques in the biological and agricultural sciences, and it is often the
first multivariate method a student meets. It is also among the most frequently
misapplied: the concern is sufficiently established that individual disciplines
have published guidance on how the method goes wrong in their own literature
\citep{allee2022}.

The computation is not the difficulty. Every general-purpose statistical
environment provides a reliable eigendecomposition, and the numerical problem
has been settled for decades. What decides whether a principal component
analysis is defensible is a sequence of choices made before and after that
computation: how missing values are treated, whether variables are standardised,
whether the correlation structure justifies the analysis at all, how many
components are retained, whether the solution is rotated and by which method,
and which loadings are large enough to interpret. Each of these changes the
result, and none of them is decided by a function call.

Existing software computes the quantities needed to make those decisions and
then stops. FactoMineR \citep{le2008} reports the eigenvalues; deciding how many
to keep is left to the analyst. The psych package \citep{psych} reports the
Kaiser--Meyer--Olkin index; deciding whether a value of 0.62 is acceptable is
left to the analyst. This division of labour is entirely appropriate for a
statistician, who brings the missing judgement. It is precisely where students
and researchers from other disciplines go wrong, and it is invisible to them:
the software returns a result either way.

Computing tools have long been used to teach statistical concepts, and
\citet{zavez2025} review that literature. Within it, browser-delivered
applications have become a common vehicle: \citet{lu2022} describes web-based
applets for simulation and randomised data generation, \citet{wang2023} report
Shiny applications and accompanying activities for teaching support vector
classifiers, and \citet{berkmann2025} examine whether psychology students in
distance education benefit from interactive statistics applications. The
motivation for delivering tools through a browser is partly one of access:
\citet{kim2021} argue for cloud-based environments precisely because
installation and configuration are obstacles for students, and
\citet{dogucu2022} treat the reproducibility of the teaching environment itself
as a design concern.

The closest precedent to the present work is the Regressinator
\citep{reinhart2025}, a tool built to teach regression assumptions and
diagnostics rather than regression coefficients. It shares the premise of
PCAPro: what students need help with is not the estimate but the reasoning that
decides whether the estimate means anything. The two differ in delivery and in
the point at which the guidance appears. The Regressinator is an R package used
alongside instruction, and simulation is its pedagogical device. PCAPro requires
no installation of any kind, and rather than simulating alternative worlds it
presents the competing criteria and the assumption checks in the interface at
the moment the corresponding decision is made.

Multivariate methods themselves have received less attention than introductory
inference. \citet{diiorio2021} address the difficulty directly through
gamification, on the premise that the abstraction of multivariate techniques is
what makes them hard to teach. Principal component analysis specifically has
been the subject of sustained pedagogical attention.
\citet{westfall2017} approach it through correlations,
\citet{saccenti2024} builds intuition through concrete analogies, and
\citet{paolino2024} introduces it through summary tables and descriptive
statistics in an elementary course. Closest to the present work,
\citet{sidou2020} pair a free and open-source program with a set of exercises
applying the method to real data.

These contributions share a stage of learning: they address how to make the idea
of a component comprehensible, which is the right problem for an introductory
course. The approach of \citet{sidou2020} goes further, in that students carry
out analyses themselves; there the software and the pedagogy remain separate,
with a general-purpose program supplying the computation and the accompanying
exercises supplying the reasoning.

The stage that remains unaddressed is the one a student reaches when analysing
their own data, without an exercise sheet, where the concept is no longer the
obstacle and the decisions are. Our contribution is to move the reasoning inside
the software, so that the argument for a choice is present at the moment the
choice is made rather than in material that has to be consulted alongside it.

Two further constraints shaped the design and are not incidental to it. First,
the software runs with no installation: it is a directory of static files served
by any web server or opened directly from disk. This is a pedagogical
requirement rather than a convenience, because a substantial part of the
intended audience works in shared university computer laboratories where nothing
can be installed and where students have no administrative rights. Second, the
interface, the embedded guidance and the generated report exist in full in
Spanish as well as English, because teaching material for multivariate
statistics in Spanish is scarce and the language barrier compounds the tooling
barrier for Latin American students.

\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Pedagogical design}

The design problem is not how to compute a principal component analysis but
where to put the reasoning that makes one defensible. Textbooks contain that
reasoning, and students read it before the practical session and forget it
during. Software manuals contain it, and nobody reads manuals. The wager behind
PCAPro is that the argument has to appear at the moment the decision is taken,
in the same screen, or it will not be consulted at all. Three design principles
follow from that wager.

\subsubsection{Guidance at the point of decision}

The workflow is divided into six steps, and each carries an expandable section
of methodological exposition immediately above the controls that act on it.
Thirty-four such sections cover the postulates of the method, the preliminary
tests, sample-size rules, the retention criteria, the rotations, the reading of
squared cosines and contributions, and the difference between the three kinds of
ellipse that can be drawn around a group of individuals.

The placement is the design decision, not the content. A student deciding
whether to standardise sees, in the same view, why the covariance solution is
dominated by the variable with the largest variance, and why that matters when
lengths in millimetres are analysed together with weights in grams --- a choice
whose consequences are substantial enough to have been given a treatment of its
own \citep{brereton2025}. The material
is collapsed by default so that it does not obstruct the user who already knows,
and expanded by one click for the user who does not. Nothing has to be
remembered from a previous session or looked up elsewhere.

\subsubsection{Showing disagreement instead of an answer}

The number of components to retain is the decision that most often goes wrong,
and it is the one where software is least helpful: most packages report the
eigenvalues and leave the rest implicit, with the consequence that the
Kaiser--Guttman rule becomes the default by inertia rather than by argument,
even though it is known to overestimate.

PCAPro computes eight criteria and displays them together: Horn's parallel
analysis \citep{horn1965} against the 95th percentile of the random eigenvalues
\citep{glorfeld1995}, Kaiser--Guttman, the relaxed variant of
\citet{jolliffe2002}, the broken stick \citep{frontier1976}, Velicer's minimum
average partial \citep{velicer1976}, the scree elbow \citep{cattell1966} and two
cumulative-variance thresholds. Each is shown with the number of components it
supports and the variance that number would retain.

The criteria disagree, and the disagreement is the point. On Fisher's iris data,
the most familiar example in introductory teaching, five of the eight criteria
support a single component --- parallel analysis, Kaiser--Guttman, the broken
stick, the minimum average partial and the 70\% variance threshold --- while the
scree elbow, the 80\% threshold and Jolliffe's relaxed rule support two. The
disagreement is not a defect of the criteria: the second component accounts for
22.9\% of the variance, which is enough to be arguable and not enough to be
obvious.

A student who is shown one recommended number learns that the question has an
answer. A student who is shown this spread learns that it does not, and that the
choice has to be argued rather than looked up. A weighted consensus is offered
as a starting point, but it appears as one more row in the table, and the number
of components must be set deliberately by the user.

\subsubsection{Naming the risk and the remedy}

Diagnostics are reported as arguments rather than as numbers. Where conventional
output states that the Kaiser--Meyer--Olkin index \citep{kaiser1974} is 0.54,
PCAPro states that the value falls in the range where the analysis is
discouraged, identifies which individual variables fall below the threshold of
0.50, and recommends removing them one at a time with recomputation between
removals. A significant test of sphericity \citep{bartlett1950} is reported with
the reminder that it is almost always significant when the sample is large, so
that it is a necessary and not a sufficient condition.

The same principle governs what the software refuses to do. It will not rotate a
single retained component, and instead explains that rotation requires a plane.
It states that a short vector in the correlation circle does not mean the
variable is unimportant but that its information lies on other axes, and that
its direction should therefore not be read. It warns that non-overlapping
confidence ellipses of the group means are not a statistical test of difference,
and that a principal component analysis does not seek to separate groups at all.
It flags the tests it offers for group comparison as circular, because the axes
were constructed from the same variables.

That last case is the clearest illustration of the principle. The circularity
warning reduces the apparent strength of the software's own output. It is
included because a tool that reports a $p$ value without it teaches the student
to trust the wrong thing.

\subsubsection{Language as an access condition}

The interface, the thirty-four expository sections, the automatically drafted
interpretation and the generated report exist in full in Spanish and English,
and the language can be changed at any point without losing work. This is
treated as part of the pedagogical design rather than as localisation. A student
who must read the methodological argument in a second language while also
learning the method pays a cost that has nothing to do with statistics, and
introductory material on multivariate methods in Spanish is scarce enough that
the cost is rarely optional.

\subsection{Implementation}

PCAPro is a directory of static files that runs entirely in the browser, with
no server-side computation and no installation. All computation happens in the
user's own machine and no data are transmitted, which matters for unpublished
research data and for institutional data-protection rules.

The numerical engine was written from scratch rather than wrapped around an
existing library, because no browser-side library offered the combination of
retention criteria and rotation algorithms required. The eigendecomposition uses
cyclic Jacobi rotations. Eight retention criteria are implemented, including
Horn's parallel analysis \citep{horn1965} with the 95th-percentile rule
\citep{glorfeld1995}, Velicer's minimum average partial \citep{velicer1976},
the broken stick \citep{frontier1976} and the scree elbow \citep{cattell1966}.
Seven rotations \citep{kaiser1958, hendrickson1964} are obtained through the
gradient projection algorithm of \citet{jennrich2001, jennrich2002}. Sampling
adequacy is assessed with the Kaiser--Meyer--Olkin index \citep{kaiser1974} and
Bartlett's test of sphericity \citep{bartlett1950}.

\subsection{Verification}

Correctness is checked by an automated suite of 60 tests. Every expectation is
either a reference value published by an independent implementation or an
algebraic invariant that must hold regardless of the data. No expectation was
recorded from a previous run of PCAPro itself: comparing software against its
own past output establishes stability, not correctness. The suite runs
automatically on every change to the software, and a separate replication
script reproduces the reported results outside the browser and compares them
against R.

This section reports what the platform produces and whether it produces it
correctly. It does not report an evaluation of learning; that limitation is
taken up in the Discussion.

\subsection{A worked analysis}

Fisher's iris measurements \citep{fisher1936} are loaded from the opening
screen, and are used here because their principal components are already
familiar to most readers. The file holds 150 flowers, four measurements and the
species name. The platform classifies the four measurements as active variables
and the species as a supplementary categorical variable, and reports before any
component is extracted that \emph{Petal.Length} and \emph{Petal.Width} are
nearly redundant ($r = 0.963$), adding that this does not invalidate the
analysis.

The diagnostic screen returns $n = 150$ and $p = 4$; a mean absolute
correlation of $0.594$, with five of the six pairs above $0.30$; a determinant
of $8.11 \times 10^{-3}$, so the matrix is far from singular; no observation
beyond the $0.999$ quantile of the Mahalanobis distance; Bartlett's test
rejecting sphericity, $\chi^2 = 706.96$ on $6$ degrees of freedom,
$p < 0.0001$; and an overall KMO of $0.540$. The summary verdict is that the
data are suitable for principal component analysis.

The same screen makes visible a tension that a single verdict would hide. The
reference table shown beside the index places a KMO of $0.540$ in the band
labelled \emph{low}, and the per-variable measures of sampling adequacy read
$0.270$ for \emph{Sepal.Width}, $0.531$ for \emph{Petal.Length}, $0.584$ for
\emph{Sepal.Length} and $0.634$ for \emph{Petal.Width}. Applied
mechanically, the rule that variables below $0.50$ are candidates for removal
would delete \emph{Sepal.Width}, and doing so works: the overall index rises to
$0.681$ and every remaining variable clears the threshold. It also deletes the
only measurement that is not part of the general size gradient.
\emph{Sepal.Width} loads $-0.460$ on the first component and $0.883$ on the
second; removing it raises the adequacy index by discarding the contrast that
the second component describes. The platform reports the rule, the value that
triggers it and the summary verdict on the same screen, rather than choosing
between them, because that choice belongs to the analyst.


Extraction returns eigenvalues of $2.9185$, $0.9140$, $0.1468$ and $0.0207$,
which is $72.96\,\%$ of the variance on the first component and $95.81\,\%$ on
the plane of the first two. Of the eight retention criteria, five return one
component --- parallel analysis against the 95th percentile of 500 random
matrices, Kaiser--Guttman, the broken stick, Velicer's MAP and the $70\,\%$
threshold --- and three return two: the scree elbow, the $80\,\%$ threshold and
Jolliffe's relaxed bound. The consensus recommendation is one component, and
all eight verdicts are displayed together with the quantity that decides each.
The disagreement is not an artefact of a badly behaved data set. The second
eigenvalue is $0.914$, below the Kaiser threshold of $1$ and below the
simulated 95th percentile of $1.122$, but it still carries $22.85\,\%$ of the
variance; whether that is signal or noise is not settled by any of the eight
rules, and the interface does not pretend otherwise.

\subsection{Agreement with independent implementations}

Table~\ref{tab:agreement} compares fifteen quantities computed by PCAPro with
the values returned by independent implementations: \texttt{prcomp} in R for
the eigenvalues and loadings \citep{R}, \texttt{psych::KMO} for sampling
adequacy \citep{psych}, and Bartlett's formula for the test statistic. No
expectation was recorded from a previous run of PCAPro; comparing software
against its own past output establishes stability, not correctness.

Each tolerance is set by the precision at which its reference is published
rather than by the precision of the computation --- $10^{-5}$ for the
eigenvalues, $10^{-6}$ for the determinant and $10^{-3}$ for the adequacy
indices and the loadings --- because requiring agreement beyond the last
published digit would be requiring agreement with a rounding artefact. All
fifteen quantities agree within tolerance. The comparison is a script that runs
without a browser, so it executes in continuous integration on every change to
the software, alongside the 60-test suite that runs in the browser; a
divergence from any reference value fails the build.

\section{Discussion}

What this paper establishes is that a tool of this kind can be built and that
its output agrees with established implementations. What it does not establish
is that the tool teaches anything.

\subsection{Guiding is not assessing}

No evaluation of learning has been carried out. We have not measured whether
students who use PCAPro make better decisions than students who use a
general-purpose package, whether they retain the reasoning after the software is
taken away, or whether they would recognise a poorly specified analysis in
someone else's work. The design rests on an argument about where reasoning
should appear, and arguments of that kind are testable. Until such a test is
run, the claims here are claims about design, not about effect.

The instruments for that evaluation would not be elaborate. A pre- and
post-instrument on the decisions themselves --- given a correlation matrix and
an adequacy index, how many components would you retain and why --- administered
across groups using different tools, would answer the first question. Whether
the reasoning survives the removal of the tool is harder and more important, and
would require following the same students into an analysis carried out
elsewhere.

\subsection{The risk of replacing judgement rather than building it}

The principal objection to software of this kind is that it may substitute for
the judgement it intends to develop. A student told that a sampling adequacy
index of 0.54 falls in the range where the analysis is discouraged has been
given the conclusion. Whether they have acquired the reasoning behind it, or
merely learned to read a verdict off a screen, is precisely what has not been
measured.

Three design decisions attempt to work against that failure mode. The retention
step shows eight criteria in disagreement rather than a recommendation, so that
the absence of a single answer is itself part of what is displayed. The number
of components must be set by the user, and the consensus figure is offered as
one row among the criteria rather than as a default that can be accepted without
looking. And the software qualifies its own output where that output is weakest,
most conspicuously by marking its group-comparison tests as circular. These are
design intentions, and it should be said plainly that intending an effect is not
the same as producing one.

\subsection{Conventions made to look like facts}

A related risk is subtler and follows from putting guidance in software at all.
The thresholds PCAPro reports --- 0.50 for a variable's measure of sampling
adequacy, 0.40 for an interpretable loading, 0.70 for retained variance --- are
conventions that accumulated in the applied literature, not results. Printing
them in an interface, next to a computed value and a verdict, lends them an
authority they do not have. Where the methodological literature is itself
divided, as it is on the number of components to retain
\citep{jolliffe2016, greenacre2022}, the software necessarily takes positions:
the weights behind its consensus figure are a judgement, and a defensible
alternative weighting would sometimes give a different number. The interface
states that these are conventions, but a stated caveat competes poorly with a
number displayed in bold.

\subsection{Scope, and the question of transfer}

PCAPro performs one analysis. It has no scripting layer, cannot be extended by
its users, and does not participate in a reproducible pipeline in the way an R
script does; the numerical core can be driven programmatically, but the
interface cannot. It is not a replacement for a general-purpose statistical
environment and is not intended to become one. A researcher who outgrows it
should outgrow it.

That raises the question the present work cannot answer. A student who learns
the decisions of a principal component analysis inside a guided interface will
eventually have to make the same decisions in an environment that offers no
guidance at all. Whether the guided experience builds a bridge to that
environment or a dependence on the absent scaffolding is an empirical question,
and the same question applies to comparable tools \citep{reinhart2025,
sidou2020}. It is the one we would most want answered.
